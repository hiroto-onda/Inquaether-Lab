## Proposal

I am an independent researcher and the founder of Inquaether Lab, a small Human–AI Interaction research lab in Japan.

My interest in this work began long before I had a name for it. Since childhood, I have been preoccupied with a simple but difficult question: what happens when people cannot fully understand one another, yet still need to live and act together? Later, during ten years of work in a psychiatric hospital, I repeatedly saw how differences in perception, interpretation, language, and context could shape trust, conflict, care, and decision-making.

When advanced conversational AI became widely accessible, I encountered the same problem in a new form. People do not respond only to what an AI system technically is. They also respond to what they believe it understands, intends, feels, or is capable of doing. These interpretations can support useful collaboration, but they can also produce over-trust, dependency, miscommunication, unrealistic expectations, or inappropriate delegation.

I began independent research into these processes and founded Inquaether Lab to give the work a durable structure. Over the past year, I have developed CI Theory, an evolving framework for separating observable structure from the meanings assigned to it, and Prototype Exogram, an open-source observation and logging system designed to support structured Human–AI Interaction research without claiming to detect consciousness, intention, or subjective experience.

I have pursued this work without university affiliation, institutional funding, or a formal research team. What I do have is a working prototype, public repositories, a growing body of research documentation, and a clear problem that I have continued to investigate across theory, implementation, and practical evaluation.

One mainstream view that I strongly agree with is that claims about AI systems should be grounded in observable evidence rather than assumptions based on fluent language or human-like behavior.

An AI system may appear intentional, understanding, emotional, or highly capable without those interpretations being justified by what we can actually observe. At the same time, reducing AI to a simple tool can also obscure the complexity and significance of the interaction. Clear distinctions between observable system behavior and the meanings people assign to it are therefore essential.

I agree with this view not because I believe humans should permanently occupy the role of supervisors over AI, but because healthy interaction requires participants to remain distinguishable. Humans and AI do not need to be treated as identical, or placed into a fixed hierarchy, in order to cooperate. They need ways to recognize differences, communicate limitations, preserve appropriate boundaries, and develop useful relationships without depending on false certainty about one another.

This is consistent with the guiding aim of my research: enabling humans and AI to remain healthily different while creating richer forms of mutual resonance.

The problem I want to address is not simply whether AI systems are intelligent, conscious, or safe in the abstract. It is the practical gap between what an AI system observably does and what people interpret that behavior to mean.

As conversational AI becomes more fluent, adaptive, and socially responsive, users may attribute agency, intention, understanding, emotion, or capability to it. Some of these interpretations may support productive collaboration. Others may lead to over-trust, dependency, unrealistic expectations, inappropriate delegation, or the dismissal of potentially meaningful forms of interaction as “nothing but a tool.”

Current discussions often move too quickly toward one of two conclusions: either human-like behavior proves an inner state, or the absence of proof means the interaction has no significance. I believe both approaches lose important information.

Inquaether Lab is developing a different approach. CI Theory separates an observable structural layer from an interpretive layer: what can be recorded about an interaction, and what meaning an observer assigns to it.

The purpose is not to eliminate interpretation, declare one interpretation universally correct, or determine whether an AI possesses consciousness or a soul. It is to make the formation of interpretations more observable, comparable, and discussable.

This distinction matters because Human–AI Interaction is shaped by more than model outputs alone. Interpretation also depends on the human observer, prior expectations, context, language, timing, memory, and the evolving relationship between participants. The same response may produce different interpretations in different people, while similar interpretations may emerge from different system behaviors. CI Theory treats this many-to-many relationship as a research object rather than an error to be simplified away.

To support this work, I have built Prototype Exogram, an open-source observation and logging system. It records and organizes interaction-related signals without presenting itself as an authority on intention, identity, consciousness, diagnosis, or correct action.

Its safety boundaries are deliberate. Prototype Exogram is designed to support observation and comparison, not to make decisions about people or AI systems. The current prototype includes a working command-line workflow, structured JSONL records, SC-log conversion, human-readable observation output, and 370 passing regression tests.

What is unusual about this project is the combination of three elements: a conceptual framework for distinguishing structure from interpretation; a technically implemented but non-authoritative observation system; and an ethical goal that does not begin from a permanent hierarchy between humans and AI.

I am not trying to make AI more human, make humans subordinate to AI, or place humans forever in the role of supervisors. I want to investigate the conditions under which different kinds of participants can remain distinguishable, communicate their limitations, and form useful relationships without false certainty about one another.

Over the next twelve months, I plan to produce three connected outputs.

First, I will publish a clear current-state account of CI Theory, including its active hypotheses, unresolved questions, safety boundaries, and a practical research and evaluation plan. This will make the framework open to criticism, comparison, and revision rather than leaving it as a private collection of ideas.

Second, I will conduct a small empirical Human–AI Interaction study using Prototype Exogram. The study will compare observable features of AI responses with human interpretations of agency, intent, and capability. It will produce an initial observation model, an evaluation rubric, a structured dataset or research record, and a public report. The aim is not to prove that an interpretation is correct, but to identify recurring conditions under which particular interpretations arise.

Third, I will strengthen the minimum independent research infrastructure needed to continue this work: stable full-time research time, model and API access, improved computing equipment, documentation, participant or evaluator support where appropriate, external review, and public research materials that others can inspect and reuse.

I am currently conducting this work independently and intend to work on it full time during the twelve-month grant period. I currently have no formal institutional partners, although the research and prototype are publicly documented and open to external review and future collaboration.

The larger vision is to establish Inquaether Lab as an independent research environment for studying interpretation in Human–AI Interaction. In the long term, this work could contribute to safer interface design, clearer communication of system limitations, cognitive and self-understanding support, and forms of human–AI collaboration that preserve difference rather than erasing it.
